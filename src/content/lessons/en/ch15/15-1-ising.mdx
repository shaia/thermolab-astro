<Hook title="Who chooses the direction?">

Hold a magnet in a flame and it stops being a magnet. Iron loses its magnetization at $770\,^\circ\text{C}$, its <Term id="curie-temperature">Curie temperature</Term>, and the loss is not a slow fading: just below that temperature the iron is magnetized, just above it is not, and the magnetization goes to zero continuously but with a vertical tangent. Let it cool again and, at the same temperature, the magnetization comes back.

It has to come back pointing *somewhere*. And here is the trouble. The energy of a piece of iron in zero field does not care which way its moments point, as long as neighbours agree: flip every moment in the sample and the energy is exactly what it was. The equations are symmetric between up and down. The sample, cooled through its Curie point, is not: it picks one. Every Boltzmann average of the magnetization is therefore exactly zero, at every temperature, yet the magnet on the bench is magnetized. **Who chooses the direction, and where did the symmetry go?**

Module 14 left a second question at the same place. Its coexistence curve *ended*: at the critical point the liquid and gas densities merged as $(1 - T/T_c)^{1/2}$, while real fluids merge as $(1 - T/T_c)^{0.32}$, an exponent the van der Waals model cannot produce. This lesson answers both with one model, so simple it fits in a line, and with a computer experiment: the <Term id="ising-model">Ising model</Term> and the <Term id="monte-carlo">Monte Carlo method</Term>. Along the way it asks what a simulation of a few thousand spins can and cannot tell us about a transition that, strictly, only happens in an infinite system.

</Hook>

<Explain title="The model, and the symmetry it breaks" id="model">

<Step fig="fig-15-1a" set={{ size: 64, temperature: 1.5, field: 0, start: 'up', rule: 'metropolis' }}>

**The model.** $N$ spins on a lattice, each $s_i = +1$ or $-1$, held at temperature $T$ by a bath and subject to a uniform field $h$, measured as an energy per spin. Each spin interacts only with its $q$ nearest neighbours ($q = 2$ on a ring, $q = 4$ on the square lattice), and the lattice is periodic: it has no edges. The spins are classical: $s_i$ is a number, not an operator.

<Formula tex="E_s = -J \sum_{\langle ij \rangle} s_i s_j - h \sum_i s_i" label="The Ising energy; the first sum counts each nearest-neighbour bond once, J > 0" />

A bond costs $-J$ when its two spins agree and $+J$ when they disagree. The ground state at $h = 0$ has every spin aligned: on the square lattice there are $2N$ bonds, so $E = -2NJ$. There are *two* ground states, all up and all down, with the same energy.

**Units.** This lesson measures energies in units of $J$, temperature as $k_BT/J$ and the field as $h/J$. Critical behaviour is the one subject in the course where the material constants genuinely scale out; that is what universality, below, claims. To quote a number for a material, put $J$ and $k_B$ back: a coupling of $J = 2\cdot10^{-21}\,\text{J}$ puts the square lattice's transition at $2.269\,J/k_B = 329\,\text{K}$.

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-15-1a" set={{ size: 64, temperature: 1.5, field: 0, start: 'random', rule: 'metropolis' }}>

**The order parameter.** The magnetization per spin

$$
m = \frac{1}{N} \sum_i s_i
$$

is the <Term id="order-parameter">order parameter</Term> of the Ising model: it is $\pm 1$ in a ground state and close to zero in a random configuration.

**The symmetry.** At $h = 0$ the energy is unchanged by flipping every spin, $s_i \to -s_i$ for all $i$, because each bond depends only on the product $s_is_j$. So a configuration and its flipped twin have the same Boltzmann weight, $p_{-s} = p_s$, and opposite magnetizations. Pairing every configuration with its twin,

$$
\langle m \rangle = \sum_s p_s\, m_s = 0 \qquad (h = 0,\ \forall N,\ \forall T)
$$

This is exact, and it is the puzzle sharpened. For any finite lattice, the canonical average of the magnetization in zero field vanishes at every temperature. If there is a magnetized phase, it cannot be found by computing $\langle m\rangle$ at $h = 0$ for finite $N$.

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-15-1a" set={{ size: 64, temperature: 1.5, field: 0.05, start: 'random', rule: 'metropolis' }}>

**Spontaneous magnetization.** What *is* computable is how the average responds to a small field, and in which order the limits are taken:

<Formula tex="m_0(T) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \lim_{N \to \infty} \langle m \rangle" label="The spontaneous magnetization: first an infinite system, then a vanishing field" />

The symmetry is <Term id="symmetry-breaking">spontaneously broken</Term> when $m_0 \neq 0$: the equations are symmetric, but an infinitesimal field applied to an infinite system selects one of two states, and the state keeps its magnetization when the field is removed. The order of limits is the whole content. Take $h \to 0$ first at finite $N$ and the answer is zero, by symmetry. Take $N \to \infty$ first, and below the transition an arbitrarily weak field (a stray field, a single defect, the history of the sample) is enough to tip the whole system one way. The figure now runs a quench in a field of only $0.05\,J$: the field's sign wins almost every time. That is who chooses the direction: a tiny bias, or the system's own history, amplified by its size.

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-15-1a" set={{ size: 8, temperature: 2.0, field: 0, start: 'up', rule: 'metropolis' }} id="ergodicity">

**The same story, told in time.** Below the transition a finite lattice in zero field does reverse its magnetization now and then: it must, or the average would not be zero. The $8 \times 8$ lattice in the figure does. But to reverse, a lattice has to pass through configurations with a wall of reversed spins running all the way across it, which costs an energy that grows with $L$, so the waiting time between reversals grows exponentially with the size. At $k_BT = 2.0\,J$ a run of $10^5$ sweeps sees an $8 \times 8$ lattice reverse dozens of times, and a $16 \times 16$ or $32 \times 32$ lattice never. For a magnet of $10^{20}$ spins the wait exceeds the age of the universe. The ensemble average is zero; the sample, for every practical purpose, is not. This is <Term id="ergodicity-breaking">ergodicity breaking</Term>: the dynamics no longer explores all the states the ensemble averages over.

**Your prediction gets its answer here.** A large lattice cooled far below the transition ends close to $+1$ or to $-1$, and which one is decided by its history. "Up and down cancel" is true of the ensemble, never of the magnet.

</Step>

</Explain>

<Explain title="Monte Carlo from detailed balance" id="metropolis">

<Step fig="fig-15-1a" set={{ size: 64, temperature: 2.5, field: 0, start: 'random', rule: 'metropolis' }}>

**Why sample.** To see any of this we need canonical averages over thousands of spins. The partition function of a $64 \times 64$ lattice is a sum of $2^{4096}$ terms, and no closed form exists outside special cases. The way around it is to *sample*: generate configurations with the right probability, and average over them. Module 11 established the target: in equilibrium with a bath, configuration $s$ occurs with probability

$$
p_s = \frac{e^{-E_s/(k_BT)}}{Z}
$$

Build a random walk through configuration space, a <Term id="markov-chain">Markov chain</Term>: from the current configuration $s$, propose flipping one spin, giving $s'$, and accept the proposal with some probability $A(s \to s')$. Proposals are symmetric: choosing which spin to try does not depend on the state. We want the walk, run long enough, to visit each configuration with probability $p_s$.

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-15-1a" set={{ size: 64, temperature: 2.5, field: 0, start: 'random', rule: 'metropolis' }}>

**Detailed balance.** A sufficient condition is that in equilibrium the flow from $s$ to $s'$ balances the flow back, for every pair:

$$
p_s\,P(s \to s') = p_{s'}\,P(s' \to s)
$$

This is <Term id="detailed-balance">detailed balance</Term>. Summing it over $s$ gives $\sum_s p_s P(s \to s') = p_{s'} \sum_s P(s' \to s) = p_{s'}$, since the walk must go somewhere: $p$ is a stationary distribution of the chain. With symmetric proposals, detailed balance asks only that

$$
\frac{A(s \to s')}{A(s' \to s)} = \frac{p_{s'}}{p_s} = e^{-\Delta E/(k_BT)}, \qquad \Delta E = E_{s'} - E_s
$$

and the <Term id="metropolis">Metropolis rule</Term> satisfies it:

<Formula tex="A(s \to s') = \min\left(1,\, e^{-\Delta E/(k_BT)}\right)" label="The Metropolis rule: accept every move that lowers the energy, and a move that raises it with probability e^(−ΔE/k_BT)" />

Check both cases. If $\Delta E > 0$, then $A(s \to s') = e^{-\Delta E/(k_BT)}$, and the reverse move lowers the energy, so $A(s' \to s) = 1$: the ratio is right. If $\Delta E \le 0$ the roles swap, and the ratio is right again. The partition function never appears: only *ratios* of Boltzmann weights do, which is why sampling succeeds where summation is hopeless.

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-15-1a" set={{ size: 64, temperature: 2.5, field: 0, start: 'random', rule: 'metropolis' }}>

**The cost of one flip.** The energy change of flipping spin $i$ needs only its neighbours:

$$
\Delta E_i = 2\,s_i \Big( J \sum_{j \in \text{nn}(i)} s_j + h \Big)
$$

On the square lattice at $h = 0$ it takes only five values, $-8J$, $-4J$, $0$, $4J$ and $8J$, so the acceptance probabilities can be computed once and looked up.

**Why a chessboard.** Updating every spin at once, each against the configuration before the update, breaks detailed balance: two neighbours that both flip each computed $\Delta E$ against a neighbour that no longer exists. The figure colours the lattice like a chessboard instead. Every neighbour of a black site is white, so with the white sites held fixed the black sites do not interact, and updating all of them at once is the same as updating them one at a time. A <Term id="mc-sweep">sweep</Term> is a black half-sweep and then a white one; each half leaves the Boltzmann distribution unchanged, so the whole sweep does too.

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-15-1a" set={{ size: 64, temperature: 1.5, field: 0, start: 'random', rule: 'glauber' }} id="mc-time">

**Monte Carlo time is not physical time.** Detailed balance fixes *where* the chain goes, its stationary distribution, and says nothing about *how fast* it gets there. Any acceptance rule with the same ratio does the same job. The <Term id="glauber-rule">Glauber rule</Term>

$$
A(s \to s') = \frac{1}{1 + e^{\Delta E/(k_BT)}}
$$

satisfies detailed balance too, accepts less often than Metropolis, and relaxes more slowly, to the same equilibrium. The figure now runs the same quench under Glauber: compare how many sweeps the energy takes to settle. Measured over eight seeds on a $64 \times 64$ lattice, after $20$ sweeps the energy per spin is $-1.80\,J$ under Metropolis and $-1.68\,J$ under Glauber; in equilibrium at $k_BT = 2.8\,J$ the two agree within their error bars. The order in which sites are visited is another free choice. A sweep is a unit of computation, and no physical constant converts it into seconds. **The chain samples equilibrium; it does not simulate the real magnet's motion.**

</Step>

</Explain>

<Explain title="Why a chain cannot order" id="chain">

<Step fig="fig-15-1b" set={{ panel: 'm', meanField: false }}>

**A ring of spins.** Before the square lattice, the simplest case: a ring of $N$ spins, started all up, at $h = 0$. The cheapest way to destroy its order is a <Term id="domain-wall">domain wall</Term>, a single bond whose two spins disagree. On a ring walls come in pairs; two of them cut the ring into an up arc and a down arc. Each wall costs $2J$, so the pair costs $\Delta E = 4J$, wherever it sits. But the first wall can sit at any of $N$ bonds and the second at any of the others, about $N^2/2$ placements, each a distinct microstate. In module 08's terms, the two-wall states have entropy $k_B \ln(N^2/2)$, and the free energy cost of breaking the order is

$$
\Delta F = \Delta E - T\,\Delta S \approx 4J - k_BT \ln\frac{N^2}{2}
$$

The energy is fixed; the entropy grows without bound with $N$. At any $T > 0$ there is an $N$ beyond which $\Delta F < 0$: walls appear spontaneously and the order is destroyed. For a single wall in an open chain the same accounting gives the form usually quoted, $\Delta F = 2J - k_BT \ln N$.

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-15-1b" set={{ panel: 'm', meanField: false }}>

<Formula tex="\frac{E}{NJ} = -\tanh\frac{J}{k_BT} \qquad m = \frac{\sinh(h/k_BT)}{\sqrt{\sinh^2(h/k_BT) + e^{-4J/k_BT}}}" label="The infinite Ising chain, exactly (transfer matrix): no spontaneous magnetization at any T > 0" />

The exact solution confirms it. The magnetization goes to zero as $h \to 0$ at every $T > 0$, and approaches $\pm1$ only as $T \to 0$. These two formulas are the lesson's first exact anchors: ThermoLab's chain reproduces them to within its error bars (a $1024$-site ring at $k_BT = J$ gives $E/(NJ) = -0.76156 \pm 0.00036$ against $-\tanh 1 = -0.76159$) before anything it says about two dimensions is believed.

**Why the argument fails in two dimensions.** A wall that cuts a square lattice in two has a length of at least $L$, so its energy grows like $2JL$. Its entropy grows like $L$ too (a wall is a path, and a path of length $\ell$ can wander in at most $3^\ell$ ways), so energy and entropy now compete on equal terms, and at low enough temperature the energy wins. The argument does not prove that the square lattice orders; it shows that nothing like the one-dimensional catastrophe forbids it. The figure shows that it does: below about $2.3\,J$ every lattice is ordered.

</Step>

</Explain>

<Explain title="Mean field, and Onsager's exact answer" id="mean-field">

<Step fig="fig-15-1b" set={{ panel: 'm', meanField: true }}>

**The mean-field equation.** Module 12 solved a single spin in a field exactly: $\langle s\rangle = \tanh(h/k_BT)$. A spin in the Ising model feels $h$ plus its neighbours. The <Term id="mean-field">mean-field approximation</Term> replaces each neighbour by its average, so spin $i$ sees an effective field $h_\text{eff} = J\sum_{j\in\text{nn}(i)}\langle s_j\rangle + h = qJm + h$ and responds to it as module 12's paramagnet. Consistency demands that the average it produces be the $m$ that was assumed:

<Formula tex="m = \tanh\left(\frac{qJm + h}{k_BT}\right) \qquad k_BT_c^\text{MF} = qJ" label="Mean field: the self-consistency equation, and its transition at h = 0" />

At $h = 0$ it always has the solution $m = 0$. It has two more, $\pm m_0$, exactly when the slope of the right side at the origin exceeds $1$, that is, below $k_BT_c^\text{MF} = qJ$. Just below it $m$ is small, and $\tanh x \approx x - x^3/3$ gives $m^2 \approx 3(T_c^\text{MF} - T)T^2/(T_c^\text{MF})^3$, so

$$
m_0 \approx \sqrt{3\left(1 - \frac{T}{T_c^\text{MF}}\right)}
$$

The magnetization vanishes as the square root of the distance to $T_c$: exponent $1/2$, the same exponent the van der Waals model gave module 14. That is not a coincidence. Both are mean-field theories, in which every particle or spin feels the average of the others, and every mean-field theory gives $1/2$.

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-15-1b" set={{ panel: 'm', meanField: true }}>

**What mean field gets wrong.** It is a good guess far from $T_c$ and a bad one near it, and it is worst in low dimensions. It predicts a transition for the chain, at $k_BT = 2J$, where the domain-wall argument proved there is none. For the square lattice it predicts $k_BT_c = 4J$: the dashed curve in the figure, still strongly magnetized where every simulated lattice has lost its order.

**Onsager's exact answer.** In 1944 Lars Onsager solved the two-dimensional Ising model at $h = 0$ exactly. The derivation is one of the great calculations of theoretical physics and lies outside this course; its results are stated without proof, and they are the lesson's reference values.

<Formula tex="k_BT_c = \frac{2J}{\ln\left(1 + \sqrt{2}\right)} \approx 2.269\,J \qquad m_0(T) = \left[1 - \sinh^{-4}\left(\frac{2J}{k_BT}\right)\right]^{1/8}" label="The square lattice, solved: Onsager's transition (1944), and the spontaneous magnetization below it (Onsager 1949, Yang 1952)" />

So mean field overestimates the transition temperature by a factor $4/2.269 = 1.763$: by $76\%$. Its error is not one of detail. Near $T_c$ a spin's neighbours are not an average: they are correlated with it, and with each other, over long distances, and mean field throws away exactly those correlations. Nor is the exponent $1/2$: Onsager and Yang's $m_0$ vanishes as $(T_c - T)^{1/8}$, far more steeply, which is the black curve's near-vertical drop.

</Step>

</Explain>

<Explain title="Critical behaviour, read from fluctuations" id="critical">

<Step fig="fig-15-1b" set={{ panel: 'chi', meanField: false }} id="identities">

**Responses are variances.** At the transition the lattice is a patchwork of domains of every size, and its responses become huge. Module 12's fluctuation identities are how a simulation sees them: in the canonical ensemble, the heat capacity per spin and the <Term id="susceptibility">susceptibility</Term> per spin are variances of the energy and of the magnetization per spin,

<Formula tex="c = \frac{N\,\text{Var}(e)}{k_BT^2} \qquad \chi = \frac{\partial\langle m\rangle}{\partial h} = \frac{N\,\text{Var}(m)}{k_BT}" label="Fluctuation identities: e = E/N and m = M/N" />

The first is module 12's $C = \text{Var}(E)/(k_BT^2)$ per spin. The second follows the same way: $h$ enters the Boltzmann weight as $e^{\beta hM}$, so $\langle M\rangle = \partial\ln Z/\partial(\beta h)$, and its derivative is $\text{Var}(M)$. No derivative has to be taken numerically: the response is read off the spontaneous fluctuations.

Below $T_c$ a finite run does not sample the two branches $\pm m$ fairly: it crosses between them rarely, so $\text{Var}(m)$ of a run measures luck. The figure applies the same identity to $|m|$, which a run *can* sample, $\chi' = N\,\text{Var}(|m|)/(k_BT)$. It is the standard estimator for finite lattices, and its peak is all this lesson uses.

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-15-1b" set={{ panel: 'chi', meanField: false }} id="finite-size">

**Critical exponents.** Near $T_c$ the singular parts of the observables follow power laws, with <Term id="critical-exponent">critical exponents</Term> conventionally called $\beta$, $\gamma$, $\alpha$ and $\nu$ (the first has nothing to do with $1/k_BT$):

$$
m_0 \propto (T_c - T)^{\beta}, \qquad \chi \propto |T - T_c|^{-\gamma}, \qquad c \propto |T - T_c|^{-\alpha}, \qquad \xi \propto |T - T_c|^{-\nu}
$$

where $\xi$ is the <Term id="correlation-length">correlation length</Term>, the size of the largest correlated patches. For the square lattice, from Onsager's solution, $\beta = 1/8$, $\gamma = 7/4$, $\nu = 1$, and the heat capacity diverges logarithmically ($\alpha = 0$). Mean field gives $\beta = 1/2$, $\gamma = 1$, $\nu = 1/2$ and a finite jump in $c$.

**Nothing diverges on a finite lattice.** $\xi$ cannot exceed $L$, so every peak in the figure is rounded and finite; its height grows with $L$, and its position drifts toward $T_c$ from above. At full scale (four seeds per size, thousands of sweeps per temperature), ThermoLab's runs put the peak of $\chi'$ at $k_BT = 2.503$, $2.416$, $2.329$ and $2.311\,J$ for $L = 8$, $16$, $32$ and $64$, with heights growing as $L^{1.76}$; the exact exponent is $\gamma/\nu = 7/4$. Extrapolating the positions linearly in $1/L$ gives $T_c = 2.272 \pm 0.009$ leaving out $L = 8$, against Onsager's $2.269$. The singularity at $T_c$ is a property of the limit $N \to \infty$.

**The transition is continuous.** Crossing $T_c$ at $h = 0$ the energy per spin is continuous (there is no latent heat, unlike module 14's boiling) and the heat capacity peaks instead: switch the figure to the heat-capacity panel. This is a <Term id="continuous-transition">continuous transition</Term>.

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-15-1b" set={{ panel: 'tau', meanField: false }} id="slowing">

**Critical slowing down.** The last panel is the autocorrelation time of $|m|$: how many sweeps the chain takes to forget its state. Deep in either phase it is about one sweep. It peaks *at* $T_c$, and it is higher for every larger lattice. A local rule changes a configuration one spin at a time, and a correlated patch of size $\xi$ takes a time growing like $\xi^z$ to rearrange, with $z \approx 2$ for Metropolis. At $T_c$ on a finite lattice $\xi \approx L$, so the autocorrelation time grows like $L^z$: ThermoLab measures $3.1$, $9.6$, $42$ and $174 \pm 38$ sweeps at $L = 8$, $16$, $32$ and $64$, a fitted $z = 1.96$. This is <Term id="critical-slowing-down">critical slowing down</Term>: the simulation is slowest exactly where the physics is most interesting. A transition is not the place where "nothing holds the system back"; it is the place where everything is correlated with everything else.

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-15-1b" set={{ panel: 'tau', meanField: false }} id="error-bars">

**An honest error bar.** That sets the standard for every number in this lesson. A trace of $n$ samples whose <Term id="autocorrelation-time">autocorrelation time</Term> is $\tau$ samples contains only about

<Formula tex="N_\text{eff} = \frac{n}{2\tau} \qquad \sigma_{\bar x} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{N_\text{eff}}}" label="Independent samples in a correlated trace, and the standard error of its mean" />

independent samples, and the standard error of its mean is $\sigma/\sqrt{N_\text{eff}}$, not $\sigma/\sqrt n$. This is module 03's correlated walk again: the $n^{-1/2}$ law survives, with $n$ replaced by the number of samples that are actually independent. The autocorrelation time is measured as $\tau = \tfrac12 + \sum_{t\ge1}\rho(t)$, where $\rho(t)$ is the correlation between samples $t$ apart, summed out to a window several times $\tau$ itself. The check is a change of seed: sixteen independent runs at $k_BT = 2.35\,J$ on a $16 \times 16$ lattice scatter by as much as their $\tau$-corrected error bars say, while the naive $\sigma/\sqrt n$ is less than half the scatter. A conclusion that changes when the seed changes was never a conclusion. Press "run again" in the figure: which features of the curves survive?

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-15-1b" set={{ panel: 'm', meanField: false }} id="universality">

**Universality.** Systems with nothing microscopic in common share critical exponents. The liquid–gas critical point of every simple fluid that has been measured (carbon dioxide, xenon, water) has $\beta \approx 0.32$–$0.33$, and so does the demixing point of binary liquid mixtures; both agree with the three-dimensional Ising model, $\beta = 0.326$. Module 14's van der Waals fluid got $1/2$ because it is a mean-field theory, not because fluids differ from magnets. What sets the exponents is the dimension of space and the symmetry of the order parameter (here, a single number with two equivalent signs), and not the forces. This is <Term id="universality">universality</Term>; the theory that explains it, the renormalization group, is beyond this course.

The dictionary between the two problems is direct. Divide a fluid into cells, each occupied by a molecule ($s_i = +1$) or empty ($s_i = -1$), with an attraction between occupied neighbours: that is an Ising model, the <Term id="lattice-gas">lattice gas</Term>. The magnetization is the density measured from its critical value, the field is the chemical potential, the line $h = 0$ below $T_c$ is the coexistence curve, and the jump in $m$ across it is the jump from gas to liquid density.

</Step>

</Explain>

<Explain title="A field swept below the transition" id="hysteresis">

<Step fig="fig-15-1c" set={{ temperature: 1.5, sweepsPerStep: 20, size: 32 }}>

**A loop.** Below $T_c$, sweep the field from strongly up to strongly down and back. The magnetization keeps its sign well past $h = 0$: a lattice with $m \approx +1$ in a negative field is metastable, module 14's superheated liquid in magnetic form. It stays there until a reversed domain large enough to grow happens to form, and then the whole lattice reverses in a few steps. On the way back the same happens with the signs exchanged, and the curve encloses a loop: <Term id="hysteresis">hysteresis</Term>. The magnetization depends on the history, not only on the present field.

Crossing $h = 0$ below $T_c$, the equilibrium magnetization jumps from $+m_0$ to $-m_0$: a <Term id="first-order-transition">first-order transition</Term>, the magnetic version of boiling, with metastable states, nucleation and hysteresis, along a line that ends at the critical point $T = T_c$, $h = 0$. Through the lattice gas, that line *is* a coexistence curve.

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-15-1c" set={{ temperature: 1.5, sweepsPerStep: 80, size: 32 }} id="slow-loop">

**Slower.** Four times as many sweeps per step: the lattice has more chances to nucleate a reversed domain at each field, so it reverses earlier and the loop narrows. Measured at $k_BT = 1.5\,J$ on a $32 \times 32$ lattice over four seeds, the area of the loop is $2.20 \pm 0.04$, $1.48 \pm 0.02$ and $1.18 \pm 0.04$ for $5$, $20$ and $80$ sweeps per step. It narrows, and it stays open: no sweep slow enough to be practical closes it.

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-15-1c" set={{ temperature: 3.0, sweepsPerStep: 20, size: 32 }} id="above-loop">

**Above the transition.** At $k_BT = 3.0\,J$ the curve passes through the origin and retraces itself: one smooth curve, the same in both directions, with an area that is only noise ($0.013$ to $0.021$ in the same runs). Above $T_c$ there is no ordered state to be stuck in, and nothing to remember.

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-15-1c" set={{ temperature: 1.5, sweepsPerStep: 20, size: 64 }} id="finite">

**Does a finite simulation ever show a phase transition?** Not in the strict sense. A phase transition is a singularity of the free energy, and on a lattice of $N$ spins the partition function is a finite sum of exponentials, an analytic function of $T$ and $h$: every observable in these figures is smooth, every peak finite, and even this hysteresis loop would close for a run long enough to wait out every nucleation. What a simulation provides is the *trend*: peaks that grow, sharpen and converge as $L$ grows, in exactly the way a singularity at $N \to \infty$ would produce, consistent with an exact solution where one exists. That is evidence, sometimes overwhelming evidence, and never proof. The singularity is a statement about a limit, and the limit is established by derivation; here, Onsager's.

<Note kind="warn">

What the model leaves out. Real magnets have quantum spins, lattice vibrations, long-range dipolar forces, anisotropy, disorder, and walls pinned by defects. Iron's Curie temperature is $1043\,\text{K}$ and nickel's $627\,\text{K}$, and the measured magnetization of both vanishes continuously with a vertical tangent, as the lesson says it must. But neither is a two-dimensional Ising magnet: they are three-dimensional, and their moments can point in any direction. Their measured $\beta$ lies near $0.36$–$0.38$: not the square lattice's $1/8$, not mean field's $1/2$, and not the three-dimensional Ising value $0.326$. Which class a system falls into is decided by its dimension and the symmetry of its order parameter.

</Note>

</Step>

<Example id="ex-15-1-1" title="Acceptance at the critical point">

On the square lattice at $h = 0$ and $k_BT = k_BT_c = 2.269\,J$, find the Metropolis acceptance probabilities of flips with $\Delta E = 4J$ and $8J$, and the Glauber probability for $\Delta E = 4J$.

<Solution>

**Given:** $k_BT = 2.269\,J$, $\Delta E = 4J$ and $8J$.

**General law:** Metropolis, $A = \min(1, e^{-\Delta E/(k_BT)})$; Glauber, $A = 1/(1 + e^{\Delta E/(k_BT)})$.

**Symbolic result:**

$$
A_\text{M}(4J) = e^{-4J/(k_BT_c)} \qquad A_\text{M}(8J) = e^{-8J/(k_BT_c)} \qquad A_\text{G}(4J) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{4J/(k_BT_c)}}
$$

**Limit check:** as $T \to \infty$ the Metropolis probabilities go to $1$ and the Glauber one to $\tfrac12$: Metropolis accepts every proposal and Glauber half of them, whatever their cost. As $T \to 0$ all three go to zero, and no move that raises the energy is ever made.

**Units:** $\Delta E$ and $k_BT$ are both in units of $J$, so every exponent is a pure number.

**Substitution:** $e^{-4/2.269} = e^{-1.763} = 0.172$; $e^{-8/2.269} = 0.029$; $1/(1 + 5.83) = 0.146$. Exactly, $e^{-2J/(k_BT_c)} = 1/(1 + \sqrt2) = \sqrt2 - 1$ by Onsager's formula, so the first is $(\sqrt2 - 1)^2 = 3 - 2\sqrt2 = 0.1716$.

**Result:** Metropolis accepts $17\%$ of the $4J$ flips and $3\%$ of the $8J$ flips; Glauber accepts $15\%$ of the $4J$ flips, less than Metropolis, as it does for every $\Delta E$. Both satisfy detailed balance; Glauber also accepts a $\Delta E = 0$ flip only half the time, where Metropolis always does.

</Solution>

</Example>

<Example id="ex-15-1-2" title="Domain walls in a ring">

A ring of spins is held at $k_BT = J$ and $h = 0$. How many sites must it have before two domain walls lower its free energy? How far apart are the walls in equilibrium?

<Solution>

**Given:** $k_BT = J$, $h = 0$; a pair of walls costs $\Delta E = 4J$ and can be placed in about $N^2/2$ ways; the exact energy per spin of the infinite chain is $E/(NJ) = -\tanh(J/k_BT)$.

**General law:** $\Delta F = \Delta E - T\Delta S$ with $\Delta S = k_B\ln(N^2/2)$; a fraction $w$ of the bonds are walls when $E/(NJ) = -(1 - w) + w = -(1 - 2w)$.

**Symbolic result:**

$$
\Delta F < 0 \iff N > \sqrt2\, e^{2J/(k_BT)} \qquad w = \frac{1 - \tanh(J/k_BT)}{2}
$$

**Limit check:** as $T \to 0$ the required $N$ grows as $e^{2J/(k_BT)}$ and $w \to 0$: walls become rare, but on a long enough ring they are always there. As $T \to \infty$, $w \to \tfrac12$: each bond is a wall half the time, and the spins are random.

**Units:** $J/(k_BT)$ is a pure number; $N$ and $w$ are counts and fractions.

**Substitution:** $N > \sqrt2\,e^2 = 10.4$; $w = (1 - 0.762)/2 = 0.119$, so a wall every $1/w = 8.4$ bonds.

**Result:** about ten sites are enough for the walls to win, and in equilibrium there is a wall roughly every eight bonds. The two estimates agree on the scale: at $k_BT = J$ order survives over a handful of sites and no further.

</Solution>

</Example>

<Example id="ex-15-1-3" title="Mean field above the true transition">

Solve the mean-field equation for the square lattice at $k_BT = 3J$ and $h = 0$ by iteration, starting from $m = 0.5$. What is the true spontaneous magnetization at this temperature?

<Solution>

**Given:** $q = 4$, $k_BT = 3J$, $h = 0$, $m_0 = 0.5$ as a starting guess.

**General law:** $m = \tanh(qJm/k_BT)$, iterated as $m \leftarrow \tanh(qJm/k_BT)$; Onsager's $k_BT_c = 2.269\,J$, with $m_0 = 0$ above it.

**Symbolic result:**

$$
m_{k+1} = \tanh\left(\frac{4}{3}\,m_k\right)
$$

**Limit check:** as $T \to 0$ the iteration goes to $m = 1$ at once, the ground state; at $k_BT \ge 4J$ the slope at the origin is at most $1$ and the only fixed point is $m = 0$: no order, in mean field.

**Units:** $qJm/k_BT$ is a pure number.

**Substitution:** $0.583$, $0.651$, $0.700$, $0.732$, $0.752$, $0.763$, …, converging to $0.776$.

**Result:** mean field says the lattice is strongly magnetized, $m = 0.776$. In truth $3J$ is above Onsager's $2.269\,J$, and $m_0 = 0$. Mean field ignores the fluctuations that, at this temperature, destroy the order it predicts.

</Solution>

</Example>

<Example id="ex-15-1-4" title="A heat capacity from a variance">

A $32 \times 32$ lattice at $k_BT = 2.5\,J$ gives an energy-per-spin trace with variance $0.0055\,J^2$. Find the heat capacity per spin.

<Solution>

**Given:** $N = 1024$, $k_BT = 2.5\,J$, $\text{Var}(e) = 0.0055\,J^2$.

**General law:** the fluctuation identity $c = N\,\text{Var}(e)/(k_BT^2)$.

**Symbolic result:**

$$
\frac{c}{k_B} = \frac{N\,\text{Var}(e)}{(k_BT)^2}
$$

**Limit check:** the variance of the energy *per spin* falls as $1/N$ for a large lattice away from $T_c$, so $c$ does not depend on the size, as a quantity per spin must not. As $T \to \infty$ the energy fluctuations stay bounded while $T^2$ grows, so $c \to 0$: a lattice at infinite temperature cannot absorb more heat.

**Units:** $J^2/J^2$: $c$ comes out in units of $k_B$.

**Substitution:** $c/k_B = 1024 \cdot 0.0055/2.5^2 = 0.90$.

**Result:** $c = 0.90\,k_B$ per spin. No derivative was taken: the response to heating is read off the spontaneous fluctuations, exactly as in module 12.

</Solution>

</Example>

<Example id="ex-15-1-5" title="An honest error bar">

A trace of $5000$ samples of $|m|$ has standard deviation $0.08$ and autocorrelation time $40$ samples. Quote the standard error of its mean, and the naive one.

<Solution>

**Given:** $n = 5000$, $\sigma = 0.08$, $\tau = 40$.

**General law:** $N_\text{eff} = n/(2\tau)$, and the standard error of the mean is $\sigma/\sqrt{N_\text{eff}}$.

**Symbolic result:**

$$
\sigma_{\bar x} = \sigma\sqrt{\frac{2\tau}{n}} \qquad \frac{\sigma_{\bar x}}{\sigma/\sqrt n} = \sqrt{2\tau}
$$

**Limit check:** for independent samples $\tau = \tfrac12$, $N_\text{eff} = n$, and the naive error is recovered. As $\tau$ approaches $n/2$ the whole trace is worth a single sample.

**Units:** $\sigma_{\bar x}$ has the units of the observable; $|m|$ is a pure number.

**Substitution:** $N_\text{eff} = 5000/80 = 62.5$; $\sigma_{\bar x} = 0.08/\sqrt{62.5} = 0.010$; naive, $0.08/\sqrt{5000} = 0.0011$; ratio $\sqrt{80} = 8.9$.

**Result:** the standard error is $0.010$. The naive $0.0011$ is nine times too small; a result quoted with it would claim a precision the run does not have.

</Solution>

</Example>

<Example id="ex-15-1-6" title="Reading Onsager and Yang">

Compute the exact spontaneous magnetization of the square lattice at $k_BT = 2J$, and compare it with mean field's $0.958$.

<Solution>

**Given:** $k_BT = 2J$, below $k_BT_c = 2.269\,J$.

**General law:** $m_0 = \left[1 - \sinh^{-4}(2J/k_BT)\right]^{1/8}$ for $T < T_c$.

**Symbolic result:**

$$
m_0(2J) = \left[1 - \sinh^{-4} 1\right]^{1/8}
$$

**Limit check:** at $T = T_c$, $\sinh(2J/k_BT_c) = 1$ and $m_0 = 0$; as $T \to 0$, $\sinh \to \infty$ and $m_0 \to 1$, the ground state.

**Units:** $2J/k_BT$ is a pure number, and so is $m_0$.

**Substitution:** $\sinh 1 = 1.1752$; $\sinh^{-4} 1 = 0.5243$; $1 - 0.5243 = 0.4757$; $0.4757^{1/8} = 0.911$.

**Result:** $m_0 = 0.911$. A $64 \times 64$ simulation gives $0.9109 \pm 0.0002$: below $T_c$ such a lattice is already, to three decimals, infinite. Mean field's $0.958$ is too high by $5\%$, even this far from the transition.

</Solution>

</Example>

<Summary title="What we learned">

- **The Ising model** is $E = -J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j - h\sum_i s_i$, with the magnetization per spin $m$ as its order parameter. At $h = 0$ its energy is symmetric under flipping every spin, so $\langle m\rangle = 0$ exactly, for every finite $N$ and every $T$.
- **Symmetry breaking is a statement about limits:** $m_0 = \lim_{h\to0^+}\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle m\rangle$. Below $T_c$ a large lattice holds one sign for longer than any run; the history, or a tiny field, chooses which. The ensemble average is zero; the magnet is not.
- **Metropolis is detailed balance made executable:** $A = \min(1, e^{-\Delta E/k_BT})$ needs only ratios of Boltzmann weights, never $Z$. Any rule with the same ratio samples the same equilibrium at a different speed, so a sweep is not a physical time.
- **One dimension cannot order** (a wall costs a fixed energy and gains $k_B\ln N$ of entropy); **mean field orders too easily** ($k_BT_c = qJ$, $76\%$ too high for the square lattice, exponent $1/2$); **Onsager's exact answer** is $k_BT_c = 2.269\,J$ with $m_0 \propto (T_c - T)^{1/8}$.
- **Responses are variances**, $c = N\text{Var}(e)/k_BT^2$ and $\chi = N\text{Var}(m)/k_BT$. On a finite lattice their peaks are finite and drift toward $T_c$ as $L$ grows; the autocorrelation time peaks at $T_c$ and grows as $L^z$.
- **An error bar uses $N_\text{eff} = n/(2\tau)$**, and is checked by changing the seed. A simulation shows trends consistent with a singularity; the singularity itself is established by derivation.
