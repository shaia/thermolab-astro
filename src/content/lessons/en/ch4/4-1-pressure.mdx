<Hook title="Violence underneath, calm on top">

Press your palm against a table and the force you feel is steady. Now press it against the air: the atmosphere pushes back on every square centimetre of your skin with about ten newtons, and that push is steady too, steady enough that a barometer built in 1643 still works today.

But the air is not a smooth fluid. It is about $2.5 \times 10^{19}$ molecules in every cubic centimetre, each moving at hundreds of metres per second, each striking your skin, rebounding and flying off. Nothing about a single collision is gentle or regular: a molecule arrives at a random moment, from a random direction, with a random speed. Every event is violent, random and discrete. How does their sum become a number so steady that we can build instruments on it, and treat it as a *property of the gas*?

The answer has nothing to do with the collisions becoming gentler. It has to do with how *many* of them there are, and it gives the course its first quantitative law of large numbers. This lesson builds the <Term id="kinetic-theory">kinetic theory</Term> of <Term id="pressure">pressure</Term> from one bouncing particle up.

</Hook>

<Explain title="From one bounce to the ideal gas law" id="derive">

<Step fig="fig-4-1a" set={{ n: 10, temperature: 300, mass: 1, side: 1 }}>

**The model.** $N$ point particles of mass $m$ in a rectangular box with sides $L_x$ and $L_y$. They fly freely and bounce off rigid walls in <Term id="elastic-collision">elastic collisions</Term>, and they never collide with each other. Their velocities are drawn at the start from the <Term id="maxwell-boltzmann">Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution</Term> at temperature $T$, then shifted so the gas as a whole is at rest and scaled so its total energy is exactly $Nk_BT$, the two-dimensional equipartition value. That is why the running average in figure 4.1a settles on the dashed line, not merely near it. The figure works in two dimensions, so the box's "volume" is its area $A = L_x L_y$ and the "area" of a wall is its length; in three dimensions every step below goes through unchanged, with $V$ in place of $A$.

**One particle, one wall.** Take the wall at $x = L_x$. A particle with velocity component $v_x$ hits it and rebounds with $-v_x$, so its momentum changed by $-2mv_x$, and by momentum conservation the wall received the <Term id="impulse">impulse</Term>

$$
\Delta p = 2m|v_x|
$$

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-4-1a" set={{ n: 10, temperature: 300, mass: 1, side: 1 }}>

Before the particle hits the same wall again it must cross the box and come back, a round trip of length $2L_x$, which takes $\Delta t = 2L_x/|v_x|$. So this one particle hands this one wall momentum at the average rate

$$
F_1 = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \frac{2m|v_x|}{2L_x/|v_x|} = \frac{mv_x^2}{L_x}
$$

Notice what happened: the awkward $|v_x|$ became $v_x^2$. A faster particle hits harder *and* more often, so its speed enters twice. The direction dropped out, and only an energy-like quantity survived: the first hint that pressure is about energy, not about impacts.

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-4-1a" set={{ n: 200, temperature: 300, mass: 1, side: 1 }}>

**All the particles.** Add up the average forces of all $N$ particles on the wall and divide by its length $L_y$:

$$
P = \frac{1}{L_y}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{mv_{x,i}^2}{L_x} = \frac{m}{A}\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{x,i}^2 = \frac{Nm\langle v_x^2\rangle}{A}
$$

where $\langle v_x^2\rangle$ is the average over the particles. This is exact for the model, and it is pure mechanics: no temperature has entered, and it would hold for any distribution of speeds.

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-4-1a" set={{ n: 200, temperature: 300, mass: 4, side: 1 }}>

**Bringing in temperature.** Temperature enters through one more statement, the <Term id="equipartition">equipartition theorem</Term>: in classical thermal equilibrium each quadratic term in the energy carries a mean energy $\tfrac12 k_B T$. For the motion along $x$,

$$
\tfrac12 m\langle v_x^2\rangle = \tfrac12 k_B T \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad m\langle v_x^2\rangle = k_B T
$$

This is a theorem of classical statistical mechanics, not a definition, and it fails when quantum effects freeze a degree of freedom out (module 16). Substituting:

<Formula tex="PA = Nk_BT \qquad PV = Nk_BT" label="The ideal gas law, in two dimensions and in three" />

The mass cancelled. At fixed $T$, a particle four times as heavy has $\langle v_x^2\rangle = k_BT/m$ four times smaller: half the speed. Each bounce delivers $2m|v_x|$, four times the mass at half the speed, so twice the punch; but at half the speed the particle returns half as often. Twice the punch, half as often: the same pressure, which is what figure 4.1a showed. This is the <Term id="ideal-gas-law">ideal gas law</Term>, and every symbol in it is now a statement about molecules.

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-4-1a" set={{ n: 200, temperature: 300, mass: 1, side: 1 }}>

**Temperature is not energy.** The derivation draws a line worth keeping sharp. Temperature fixes the mean energy *per particle*: in two dimensions $\langle E\rangle = k_BT$, in three $\tfrac32 k_BT$. The <Term id="internal-energy">internal energy</Term> is the *total*, $U = N\langle E\rangle$. Two containers at the same temperature have the same mean energy per particle whatever their size; the bigger one simply has more particles, and more internal energy.

<Note kind="warn">

What we have and have not shown. We derived the ideal gas law from mechanics **plus** equipartition, for point particles that never interact. We did not derive it for real gases: at high density the particles' own volume and their mutual attraction both matter, and the law acquires corrections (module 14). Historically the ideal gas law is an empirical law; it is a theorem only inside this model.

</Note>

</Step>

</Explain>

<Explain title="Why the gauge is steady" id="steady">

<Step fig="fig-4-1b" set={{ n: 10 }}>

**Two kinds of jitter.** The pale line in figure 4.1a jitters because a short window of time catches only a handful of impacts. More particles, or a longer window, put more impacts in each window, and the jitter of an average over $M$ independent impacts shrinks in proportion to $M^{-1/2}$. A real gauge averages an astronomical number of them: air strikes each square centimetre about $3\cdot10^{23}$ times a second (example 3 below), so a gauge of that size reading once a millisecond averages some $3\cdot10^{20}$ impacts, and its jitter is of order $10^{-10}$. That is why the gauge is steady.

Figure 4.1b asks a second question, about the gas rather than the gauge. Average each gas for so long that the window jitter is gone, and prepare many gases at the same temperature: do their pressures agree? Each bar counts gases whose long-time pressure lands in that range. With ten particles, most land between half and one and a half times the ideal value: a <Term id="fluctuation">fluctuation</Term> of about thirty percent.

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-4-1b" set={{ n: 1000 }}>

**Where the $N^{-1/2}$ comes from.** The derivation gave $P = Nm\langle v_x^2\rangle/A$ for one wall. Averaging over all four walls of a square box, the long-time pressure of one gas is

$$
P = \frac{m}{2A}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(v_{x,i}^2 + v_{y,i}^2\right)
$$

a sum of $2N$ squared velocity components. Each component is an independent Gaussian, so the sum has mean $2N$ times the mean of one square, while its standard deviation grows only as $\sqrt{2N}$: for squared Gaussians the relative spread of the sum is $\sqrt{2/(2N)}$. Therefore

<Formula tex="\frac{\sigma_P}{\langle P\rangle} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}" label="Spread of the long-time pressure, N particles in two dimensions" />

Measured in figure 4.1b, the points fall on the line: with a thousand particles the spread is about three percent. In three dimensions the same count gives $\sqrt{2/(3N)}$, and a sample that also trades molecules with its surroundings has yet another factor of order one; what never changes is the $N^{-1/2}$. The spread depends on how many particles share the energy, not on the temperature or on their mass.

</Step>

<Step fig="fig-4-1b" set={{ n: 10000 }} id="mole">

Ten thousand particles: one percent. The law does not stop. A mole holds $6\times10^{23}$ particles, and $N^{-1/2} \approx 1.3\times10^{-12}$: a spread in the twelfth decimal place, far below anything an instrument has ever resolved. Both answers to the puzzle have the same shape: a sum of very many independent random contributions, whose relative spread falls as one over the square root of their number. For the gauge's reading the contributions are impacts; for the pressure of the gas itself they are molecules. Either way the collisions never became gentle; there are simply so many of them that the relative jitter fell below the noise floor of reality.

A quantity is **macroscopic** when its relative fluctuation is negligible for the system at hand. That makes "macroscopic" a statement about $N$, not about size in metres: a droplet of $10^9$ molecules has a perfectly well-defined pressure, while ten molecules in a trap do not.

<Note kind="warn">

What the simulation proves, and what it does not. Numerical agreement supports the derivation; it does not establish it. These particles never collide with each other, so the model cannot *reach* a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution, only preserve one that was imposed at the start. The figures show that *if* a gas has that distribution, its pressure follows. Whether a real gas gets there is a separate claim, for modules 8 and 11.

</Note>

</Step>

</Explain>

<Example id="ex-4-1-1" title="Two containers at the same temperature">

Container A holds $1.00\,\text{mol}$ of helium at $300\,\text{K}$ in $24.9\,\text{L}$. Container B holds $2.00\,\text{mol}$ of helium at $300\,\text{K}$ in $49.8\,\text{L}$.

(a) Find the pressure in each container.

(b) Find the mean kinetic energy per atom, and the internal energy, of each.

<Solution>

**Given:** $N_A = 6.022\cdot10^{23}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$, $k_B = 1.381\cdot10^{-23}\,\text{J/K}$, $T = 300\,\text{K}$; A: $n = 1.00\,\text{mol}$, $V = 2.49\cdot10^{-2}\,\text{m}^3$; B: $n = 2.00\,\text{mol}$, $V = 4.98\cdot10^{-2}\,\text{m}^3$.

**General law:** the ideal gas law in three dimensions, $PV = Nk_BT$, and equipartition: three quadratic terms per atom, so $\langle E\rangle = \tfrac32 k_BT$ and $U = \tfrac32 Nk_BT$.

**Symbolic result:**

$$
P = \frac{Nk_BT}{V} \qquad \langle E\rangle = \tfrac32 k_BT \qquad U = \tfrac32 Nk_BT
$$

**Limit check:** as $V \to \infty$ at fixed $N$ the pressure goes to zero, as it must for a gas spread infinitely thin. At fixed $N/V$ the pressure does not depend on the size of the container, which is why doubling $N$ and $V$ together changes nothing. $\langle E\rangle$ depends on $T$ only, so it cannot tell a large container from a small one.

**Units:** $\text{J/K}\cdot\text{K}/\text{m}^3 = \text{J}/\text{m}^3 = \text{N}/\text{m}^2 = \text{Pa}$.

**Substitution:** A: $N = 6.022\cdot10^{23}$, $P = 6.022\cdot10^{23}\cdot1.381\cdot10^{-23}\cdot300/2.49\cdot10^{-2} = 1.00\cdot10^5\,\text{Pa}$. B: twice the atoms in twice the volume, the same $1.00\cdot10^5\,\text{Pa}$. In both, $\langle E\rangle = 1.5\cdot1.381\cdot10^{-23}\cdot300 = 6.21\cdot10^{-21}\,\text{J}$. $U_A = 6.022\cdot10^{23}\cdot6.21\cdot10^{-21} = 3.74\cdot10^3\,\text{J}$ and $U_B = 7.48\cdot10^3\,\text{J}$.

**Result:** (a) $1.00\cdot10^5\,\text{Pa}$ in both. (b) The same mean energy per atom, $6.21\cdot10^{-21}\,\text{J}$, because the temperature is the same; B has twice the internal energy, $7.48\,\text{kJ}$ against $3.74\,\text{kJ}$, because it has twice the atoms.

</Solution>

</Example>

<Example id="ex-4-1-2" title="How small before the pressure wavers?">

A pressure sensor can resolve a relative change of $1\%$. How many molecules must a sample of air hold for its pressure to fluctuate by that much? How large is that sample, at $1.013\cdot10^5\,\text{Pa}$ and $300\,\text{K}$?

<Solution>

**Given:** $\sigma_P/\langle P\rangle = 0.01$, $P = 1.013\cdot10^5\,\text{Pa}$, $T = 300\,\text{K}$, $k_B = 1.381\cdot10^{-23}\,\text{J/K}$.

**General law:** $\sigma_P/\langle P\rangle \approx N^{-1/2}$, up to a factor of order one that depends on the dimension and on whether the sample trades molecules with the air around it; and the ideal gas law $PV = Nk_BT$ for the volume that holds $N$ molecules.

**Symbolic result:**

$$
N = \left(\frac{\sigma_P}{\langle P\rangle}\right)^{-2} \qquad V = \frac{Nk_BT}{P} \qquad \ell = V^{1/3}
$$

**Limit check:** as the resolution demanded goes to zero, $N$ grows without bound: any finite sample fluctuates, but the fluctuation vanishes only for an infinite one. At the other end, $N = 1$ gives a $100\%$ fluctuation: one molecule exerts no steady pressure at all, which is exactly the puzzle this lesson started from.

**Units:** $\text{J/K}\cdot\text{K}/\text{Pa} = \text{J}/(\text{N}/\text{m}^2) = \text{m}^3$.

**Substitution:** $N = 0.01^{-2} = 1.0\cdot10^4$ molecules. $V = 1.0\cdot10^4\cdot1.381\cdot10^{-23}\cdot300/1.013\cdot10^5 = 4.09\cdot10^{-22}\,\text{m}^3$, a cube of side $\ell = 7.4\cdot10^{-8}\,\text{m}$.

**Result:** about $10^4$ molecules, a cube of air about $74\,\text{nm}$ across. The factor of order one moves this little: $\ell$ grows only as $N^{1/3}$, so it changes the side by under twenty percent. Any sample a person can see is enormously larger, which is why no one has ever watched the air pressure twitch.

</Solution>

</Example>

<Example id="ex-4-1-3" title="A hail on every square centimetre">

Air at $300\,\text{K}$ and $1.013\cdot10^5\,\text{Pa}$ is mostly nitrogen ($28.0\,u$, with $u = 1.661\cdot10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$). The number of molecules striking a wall per unit area per second is $\Phi = \tfrac14 n\bar v$, where $\bar v = \sqrt{8k_BT/(\pi m)}$ is the mean speed.

(a) How many molecules strike one square centimetre of your skin each second?

(b) What momentum does one impact deliver, on average? Why is it larger than $2m\sqrt{\langle v_x^2\rangle}$?

<Solution>

**Given:** $T = 300\,\text{K}$, $P = 1.013\cdot10^5\,\text{Pa}$, $m = 28.0\cdot1.661\cdot10^{-27} = 4.65\cdot10^{-26}\,\text{kg}$, $k_B = 1.381\cdot10^{-23}\,\text{J/K}$.

**General law:** $n = P/(k_BT)$ from the ideal gas law; the force per area is the impact rate per area times the mean momentum per impact, so $\overline{\Delta p} = P/\Phi$.

**Symbolic result:**

$$
\Phi = \frac14\,\frac{P}{k_BT}\sqrt{\frac{8k_BT}{\pi m}} \qquad \overline{\Delta p} = \frac{P}{\Phi} = \sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}
$$

**Limit check:** as $T \to 0$ at fixed $P$, $\Phi \propto T^{-1/2}$ grows while $\overline{\Delta p} \propto T^{1/2}$ shrinks: a cold gas at the same pressure pushes with more, gentler impacts. As $m \to \infty$ at fixed $T$, each impact carries more momentum and they come more rarely, with $P$ unchanged.

**Units:** $\Phi$: $\text{m}^{-3}\cdot\text{m/s} = \text{m}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}$; $\overline{\Delta p}$: $\sqrt{\text{kg}\cdot\text{J}} = \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$.

**Substitution:** $n = 2.45\cdot10^{25}\,\text{m}^{-3}$, $\bar v = 476\,\text{m/s}$, $\Phi = 2.91\cdot10^{27}\,\text{m}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1} = 2.91\cdot10^{23}\,\text{cm}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}$. $\overline{\Delta p} = 3.48\cdot10^{-23}\,\text{kg·m/s}$, against $2m\sqrt{\langle v_x^2\rangle} = 2.78\cdot10^{-23}\,\text{kg·m/s}$.

**Result:** (a) About $3\cdot10^{23}$ impacts per square centimetre per second. (b) $3.48\cdot10^{-23}\,\text{kg·m/s}$ per impact. It is larger because fast molecules reach the wall more often, so the molecules that do strike are faster than the average one.

</Solution>

</Example>

<Example id="ex-4-1-4" title="A balloon in the freezer">

A party balloon holds $5.0\,\text{L}$ of helium at $300\,\text{K}$, at atmospheric pressure. It is put in a freezer at $255\,\text{K}$; its skin keeps the pressure close to atmospheric.

(a) What is its volume once it has cooled?

(b) By what fraction has the mean kinetic energy per atom fallen? Has the number of atoms changed?

<Solution>

**Given:** $V_1 = 5.0\,\text{L}$, $T_1 = 300\,\text{K}$, $T_2 = 255\,\text{K}$, $P$ and $N$ fixed.

**General law:** $PV = Nk_BT$ and $\langle E\rangle = \tfrac32 k_BT$.

**Symbolic result:**

$$
V_2 = V_1\,\frac{T_2}{T_1} \qquad \frac{\langle E\rangle_2}{\langle E\rangle_1} = \frac{T_2}{T_1}
$$

**Limit check:** $T_2 = T_1$ leaves everything unchanged; as $T_2 \to 0$ the volume would shrink to zero, but long before that the helium stops being an ideal gas (see the last example, on quantum helium).

**Units:** a ratio of temperatures is dimensionless, so $V_2$ is in litres like $V_1$.

**Substitution:** $V_2 = 5.0\cdot255/300 = 4.3\,\text{L}$. $\langle E\rangle$ falls from $6.21\cdot10^{-21}\,\text{J}$ to $5.28\cdot10^{-21}\,\text{J}$, by $15\%$.

**Result:** (a) $4.3\,\text{L}$. (b) $15\%$ less energy per atom; the number of atoms is unchanged. The balloon shrinks because each atom pushes more gently and less often, not because any atoms left.

</Solution>

</Example>

<Example id="ex-4-1-5" title="One big sample or a hundred small ones?">

Sensor A samples the pressure of $10^4$ molecules and averages a hundred independent readings. Sensor B takes a single reading of $10^6$ molecules. Which reading is steadier?

<Solution>

**Given:** A: $N_A = 10^4$, $k = 100$ independent readings averaged; B: $N_B = 10^6$, one reading.

**General law:** one reading of $N$ independent molecules has $\sigma_P/\langle P\rangle \approx N^{-1/2}$; the average of $k$ independent readings has a spread $k^{-1/2}$ times that of one.

**Symbolic result:**

$$
\left(\frac{\sigma_P}{\langle P\rangle}\right)_A = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N_A}}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt k} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{kN_A}} \qquad \left(\frac{\sigma_P}{\langle P\rangle}\right)_B = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N_B}}
$$

**Limit check:** $k = 1$ gives back a single reading of $N_A$. In both cases what counts is the total number of independent contributions, $kN_A$ or $N_B$: the $N^{-1/2}$ law does not care whether they come at once or one sample after another.

**Units:** both are pure numbers.

**Substitution:** A: $1/\sqrt{100\cdot10^4} = 10^{-3}$. B: $1/\sqrt{10^6} = 10^{-3}$.

**Result:** they are equally steady, $10^{-3}$, because both average over $10^6$ independent contributions.

</Solution>

</Example>

<Example id="ex-4-1-6" title="When is helium a quantum gas?">

The model fails when the thermal wavelength $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}$ of the atoms becomes comparable with their spacing $n^{-1/3}$. Compare helium gas ($4.003\,u$) at atmospheric pressure at $300\,\text{K}$ and at its boiling point, $4.2\,\text{K}$. Take $h = 6.626\cdot10^{-34}\,\text{J·s}$.

<Solution>

**Given:** $m = 4.003\cdot1.661\cdot10^{-27} = 6.65\cdot10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$, $P = 1.013\cdot10^5\,\text{Pa}$, $T = 300\,\text{K}$ and $4.2\,\text{K}$.

**General law:** $n = P/(k_BT)$, so the spacing is $(k_BT/P)^{1/3}$, and $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}$.

**Symbolic result:**

$$
\frac{\lambda}{n^{-1/3}} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}}\left(\frac{P}{k_BT}\right)^{1/3} \propto T^{-5/6}
$$

**Limit check:** as $T \to \infty$ the ratio goes to zero and the gas is classical; cooling at fixed pressure makes the atoms both slower (longer $\lambda$) and closer, so the ratio grows fast.

**Units:** $\text{J·s}/\sqrt{\text{kg}\cdot\text{J}} = \text{m}$, and $(\text{J}/\text{Pa})^{1/3} = (\text{m}^3)^{1/3} = \text{m}$: the ratio is a pure number.

**Substitution:** at $300\,\text{K}$ the ratio is $1.5\cdot10^{-2}$. At $4.2\,\text{K}$: $n = 1.75\cdot10^{27}\,\text{m}^{-3}$, spacing $8.3\cdot10^{-10}\,\text{m}$, $\lambda = 4.3\cdot10^{-10}\,\text{m}$, a ratio of $0.51$.

**Result:** at room temperature helium is safely classical ($\lambda$ is $1.5\%$ of the spacing); at its boiling point $\lambda$ is half the spacing, and quantum statistics can no longer be ignored (module 17).

</Solution>

</Example>

<Summary title="What we learned">

- **Pressure is momentum transfer.** A bounce hands the wall $2m|v_x|$; a particle returns every $2L_x/|v_x|$; so it pushes with an average force $mv_x^2/L_x$. Summed over the particles, $PA = Nm\langle v_x^2\rangle$, exactly, for any distribution of speeds.
- **Temperature enters through equipartition**, $m\langle v_x^2\rangle = k_BT$, which turns that into the ideal gas law, $PA = Nk_BT$ in two dimensions and $PV = Nk_BT$ in three. The mass cancels: heavier particles hit harder and less often.
- **Temperature is energy per particle; internal energy is the total.** Same temperature, same mean energy per particle, whatever the size of the container.
- **The pressure is steady because $N$ is large**, not because collisions are gentle. Its relative fluctuation is $N^{-1/2}$: thirty percent for ten particles, one percent for ten thousand, $10^{-12}$ for a mole.
- **When the model fails:** at high density (particle size and attraction matter, module 14), at low temperature (quantum statistics, module 17), and wherever the particles interact strongly. The simulation preserves a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution; it cannot show that a real gas reaches one.
